# Calibration Robustness of Photometric Star, Galaxy, and Quasar Classifiers Under Magnitude Shift

**Joshua Anojulu**  
Texas Academy of Mathematics and Science  
University of North Texas, Denton, TX, USA  

## Abstract

Modern photometric surveys classify far more astronomical sources than spectroscopy can confirm, and they rely on the class probabilities of machine-learning classifiers to build clean samples and prioritize follow-up. A probability is useful only if it is calibrated: an object assigned 90% confidence should belong to the predicted class about 90% of the time. Accuracy loss toward faint magnitudes is well documented for photometric classification, but how probability calibration behaves under this shift has not, to our knowledge, been characterized for the star/galaxy/quasar problem. We measure the calibration of four common classifiers on SDSS photometry, track how calibration changes with source magnitude, and test whether post-hoc recalibration (Platt scaling, isotonic regression, temperature scaling) fit on bright sources remains valid on faint ones. The classifiers and recalibration methods are all standard; the contribution is the magnitude-resolved measurement, not a new technique. On a sample of 499,995 SDSS sources, the random-forest, gradient-boosted, and neural-network classifiers prove well calibrated (expected calibration error, ECE, of 0.003–0.005) and, despite the expectation set by their faint-end accuracy loss, remain so across the full magnitude range; calibration follows model capability, and only the weaker logistic-regression model is miscalibrated (ECE 0.077). Recalibration fit on bright sources transfers poorly to faint ones: Platt scaling inflates faint-bin ECE by roughly an order of magnitude, while temperature scaling leaves it near the uncalibrated value. For the well-calibrated models, a probability-thresholded quasar sample is as pure as its probabilities promise at every magnitude, and the faint-end cost falls on completeness. Within the magnitude range and target-selected sample studied here, capable photometric classifiers can therefore supply trustworthy selection probabilities, with the risks concentrated in weak models and in naive recalibration. These findings bear on probabilistic source typing in LSST-era pipelines.

*Index Terms:* probability calibration, expected calibration error, photometric classification, distribution shift, post-hoc recalibration, sample selection, sky surveys.

## I. Introduction

Wide-field photometric surveys catalog far more astronomical sources than spectroscopy can confirm. To type sources as stars, galaxies, or quasars at this scale, surveys rely on supervised machine-learning classifiers trained on the comparatively small set of spectroscopically confirmed objects; one recent catalog applies such a model to more than one hundred million SDSS sources without spectra [10]. As the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) expands catalog volumes by orders of magnitude while spectroscopic follow-up stays scarce, probabilistic photometric classification is becoming the primary means of source typing. Supervised classification now spans astronomy, from time-domain stellar evolutionary-state typing [8] to survey-scale source classification.

Such classifiers report a probability for each class along with a predicted label, and downstream analyses threshold or weight on these probabilities to assemble pure samples and to prioritize follow-up. A probability is trustworthy only if it is calibrated: among all objects assigned a given confidence, the fraction that belong to the predicted class should equal that confidence. Calibration is distinct from accuracy: a classifier can be accurate yet over- or under-confident, and contemporary classifiers often miscalibrate in ways that vary by model family [1], [2].

Two independent lines of research bear on whether these probabilities can be trusted at faint magnitudes. Within astronomy, classifier accuracy is known to degrade toward faint magnitudes, where labeled training data are sparse and unrepresentative (a consequence of sample-selection, or Malmquist, bias); magnitude-resolved analyses of photometric star/galaxy/quasar classification confirm that faint sources are misclassified at higher rates [9]. Within machine learning, probability calibration is known to deteriorate under distribution shift, and post-hoc recalibration fit on in-distribution data loses validity as the shift grows [5]. Calibration is not new to astronomical classification. Richards et al. built an explicitly calibrated probabilistic classification catalog of 50,124 variable sources, arguing that calibrated posteriors are precisely what allow purity and completeness to be treated rigorously when follow-up resources are scarce [13]. Since then, reliability diagrams have been reported for gamma-ray source classifiers [6], temperature scaling has been applied to supernova classification [7], and calibration metrics have appeared for star/galaxy/quasar classifiers themselves [12]. Each of these establishes calibration at a single aggregate operating point. How that calibration behaves under the magnitude shift that erodes accuracy, and whether a recalibration fit at bright magnitudes transfers to faint ones, has to our knowledge not been examined.

Using SDSS photometry, we (i) measure the calibration of four widely used classifiers on an independent test set, (ii) characterize how calibration changes as a function of source magnitude, and (iii) test whether a recalibration map fit on bright sources remains valid when applied to faint ones. The contribution is empirical rather than methodological: we introduce no new classifier and no new calibration method, and instead characterize how well established ones hold up along an axis, source magnitude, that survey application makes unavoidable. Where prior work establishes that a calibrated probabilistic catalog can be built [13], we ask whether that calibration survives the magnitude shift, and what a probability-thresholded selection is consequently worth at each magnitude. We find robustness: the three higher-accuracy classifiers are well calibrated and stay so across magnitude. The hazards sit elsewhere, in a miscalibrated weak model and in bright-fit recalibration that degrades faint-end calibration, while the faint regime's cost falls on sample completeness.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II details the data, classifiers, calibration metrics, and the magnitude-shift protocol; Section III reports results; and Section IV discusses limitations and implications for forthcoming surveys.

## II. Methods

### A. Data and Features

Labeled data are drawn from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), using spectroscopic classifications as ground truth. Data Release 17 (DR17) is used for its well-characterized legacy spectroscopy and direct comparability with prior photometric-classification studies; the procedure applies unchanged to later releases. Spectroscopically confirmed objects with reliable redshifts (zWarning = 0) are selected from the clean SpecObj view and matched to their primary, clean photometric counterparts in PhotoObjAll via bestObjID (clean = 1, mode = 1), retaining only sources with class in {STAR, GALAXY, QSO}.

Each source is described by its five dereddened model magnitudes (u, g, r, i, z) and the four adjacent colors (u−g, g−r, r−i, i−z). We exclude the spectroscopic redshift from the feature set: it is a near-perfect proxy for quasar identity and would dominate the model and trivialize the calibration analysis. We also withhold the dereddened r-band magnitude and reserve it to define the magnitude shift (Section II-F). The final sample comprises 499,995 objects: 295,200 galaxies (59%), 108,857 stars (22%), and 95,938 quasars (19%). This class distribution is set by SDSS spectroscopic targeting and, like the magnitude distribution, is non-uniform (Section IV).

### B. Data Partitioning

Sources are partitioned by object into three disjoint sets: a training set, a calibration set, and a test set (60/20/20, stratified by class to preserve class proportions; here 299,997 / 99,999 / 99,999). The classifier is fit on the training set; any post-hoc recalibration map (Section II-E) is fit on the calibration set alone; and all reported calibration and accuracy metrics are computed on the held-out test set. This separation is essential: a recalibration map fit on training-set scores, which are overfit and overconfident, or on the test set would invalidate the measurement. To quantify sampling variability, we repeat the entire fit-calibrate-evaluate protocol over 20 independent class-stratified splits and report every metric as the mean with a 95% confidence interval across those splits.

### C. Classifiers

Four classifiers spanning distinct model families and known calibration behaviors are compared: multinomial logistic regression, a random forest, a gradient-boosted decision-tree ensemble, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP). We select each model's hyperparameters by five-fold cross-validated grid search (minimizing negative log-likelihood) on the training split: the random forest over tree count and maximum depth; the gradient-boosted ensemble over learning rate and iteration count; the MLP over hidden-layer width and L2 penalty; and logistic regression over its inverse regularization strength. The selected configuration (here a depth-limited 300-tree forest and a 128-64 MLP) is held fixed across all repeated splits. Because selection uses a single reference split, the reported confidence intervals reflect split-to-split variability only, not hyperparameter-selection uncertainty. Each model emits a class-probability vector (via predict_proba for the tree- and regression-based models and a softmax output layer for the MLP); the predicted class is the argmax of that vector.

### D. Calibration Metrics

Calibration is assessed graphically with reliability diagrams, which plot empirical accuracy against predicted confidence in bins of confidence; the identity line denotes perfect calibration, and deviations above (below) it indicate under- (over-) confidence. Because the task is three-class, we summarize top-label calibration, the confidence of the predicted class max_k p_k, and also report per-class one-vs-rest calibration errors.

Calibration is quantified by the Expected Calibration Error (ECE),

$$\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N}\,\bigl|\,\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\,\bigr|,$$

the sample-weighted mean gap between accuracy and confidence across M confidence bins, together with the Maximum Calibration Error (the largest such gap). Two proper scoring rules that jointly reward calibration and sharpness are also reported: the multiclass Brier score [4] (mean squared error between the predicted probability vector and the one-hot label) and the negative log-likelihood. Because ECE is sensitive to the binning scheme [3], it is reported with both equal-width and equal-mass (quantile) bins and at two bin counts (15 and 10).

### E. Recalibration Methods

Three standard post-hoc recalibration methods, each fit on the calibration set, are evaluated against the uncalibrated baseline: Platt (sigmoid) scaling and isotonic regression, both applied one-vs-rest for the multiclass setting, and temperature scaling. Temperature scaling divides the logits by a single scalar T > 0 before the softmax,

$$p_k = \mathrm{softmax}(z_k / T),$$

with T chosen to minimize the negative log-likelihood on the calibration set; because it does not alter the argmax, it leaves classification accuracy unchanged, a property used as a consistency check. Calibration metrics and temperature scaling are implemented directly. Because the four models do not all expose logits, temperature scaling is applied to log-probabilities as pseudo-logits; for the tree ensembles, whose predicted probabilities can equal zero and are clipped before the logarithm, this approximates rather than reproduces logit rescaling. Platt (sigmoid) and isotonic recalibration use scikit-learn's CalibratedClassifierCV applied to a frozen, already-fitted base estimator so that the recalibration map is fit only on the calibration set; in the multiclass setting this wrapper fits each class one-vs-rest and renormalizes the outputs, a step that can itself perturb probabilities that arrive well calibrated. The transfer results of Section III-C are therefore results for these standard implementations.

### F. Magnitude-Shift Protocol

The central experiment tests whether calibration survives the magnitude shift that characterizes real survey application. The test set is divided into five dereddened-r magnitude bins (edges at 14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 22), with a binary bright/faint split at r = 19. Calibration metrics are computed within each magnitude bin for (i) the uncalibrated model, (ii) a recalibration map fit on a representative (all-magnitude) calibration set, and (iii) a recalibration map fit on bright sources only. Comparing (iii) across bins isolates the transfer gap: the degree to which a recalibration learned on bright sources fails on faint ones. Because the SDSS spectroscopic sample is magnitude-limited and target-selected, the faint regime here is not a representative faint population; Section IV addresses this limitation and its bearing on interpretation.

Because class composition itself varies across the magnitude bins, classwise calibration error is also reported within each bin: for each class, the predicted probability of that class is compared, one-versus-rest, to the empirical frequency of that class. Unlike aggregate top-label ECE, this holds the class mix out of the metric, so a flat classwise trend would show that any magnitude robustness belongs to the calibration itself and is not inherited from the shifting class proportions.

### G. Decision-Relevant Selection Quality

To translate calibration error into a concrete observational cost, we also measure the quality of a probability-thresholded sample. Taking the quasar class as the science target, a candidate sample is formed from all test objects with P(QSO) ≥ τ (default τ = 0.9). For this sample the achieved purity (the true fraction of quasars among the selected objects) and completeness (the fraction of all true quasars recovered) are computed within each magnitude bin and compared against the promised purity, the mean predicted P(QSO) of the selected objects, which for a perfectly calibrated model equals the achieved purity. The gap between promised and achieved purity is thus a direct, decision-relevant measure of miscalibration: the degree to which a probability-selected sample is less pure than its probabilities claim. This gap is evaluated by magnitude bin for the uncalibrated model and after representative- and bright-only recalibration. The principle that well-calibrated probabilities allow sample purity and completeness to be read directly from a threshold is established [11]; the contribution here is to test it across magnitude and under recalibration.

## III. Results

### A. Baseline Calibration

On the held-out test set (99,999 objects), the random forest, gradient-boosted trees, and MLP reach comparable accuracy (0.92–0.93) and are all well calibrated, with ECE between 0.003 and 0.005 (Table I; Fig. 1); 95% confidence intervals over the 20 splits are narrow (ECE half-widths ≤ 0.001); because repeated splits of one catalog are correlated, such intervals can understate sampling uncertainty, so we also report per-split consistency: every split ranks all three strong models ahead of logistic regression and HistGB ahead of the random forest, while HistGB and the MLP trade places from split to split. HistGB and the MLP are the best calibrated (ECE 0.003 and 0.004), with the random forest close behind (0.005); all three show small maximum calibration errors (0.045–0.062). Logistic regression is the outlier on both axes: least accurate (0.81) and least calibrated by an order of magnitude (ECE 0.077, MCE 0.161), reflecting the limited capacity of a linear model on this feature space. Calibration on this task therefore tracks model capability.

### B. Calibration as a Function of Magnitude

Figure 2 resolves calibration across five r-band magnitude bins. For the three strong models, top-label ECE stays low and flat from the bright end to the faint end: random forest 0.006 (brightest) to 0.004 (faintest), peaking near 0.012 in between; HistGB 0.003–0.006 throughout; MLP 0.003 to 0.006. None of the three degrades toward faint sources, so the expected erosion of calibration under magnitude shift does not appear for capable models. Logistic regression behaves differently: its ECE is highest in the brightest bin (0.17) and decreases toward the faint end (0.03), a trend opposite to the hypothesis and attributable in part to the changing class composition across magnitude (Section IV). Accuracy for the three strong models stays high across all bins; the dominant magnitude effect is the harder problem of recovering faint quasars, addressed in Section III-D.

To separate this from the varying class mix across bins, classwise calibration error (one class versus the rest, which holds the class proportions out of the metric) is also computed per bin. For the random forest it is 0.005 in the brightest bin and 0.004 in the faintest (peaking near 0.009 in between), and the individual star, galaxy, and quasar terms each stay below 0.011 at every magnitude; HistGB and the MLP behave the same way. The flat classwise trend confirms that the magnitude robustness is intrinsic to the calibration rather than an artifact of the changing class proportions.

*Fig. 2. Top-label ECE as a function of r-band magnitude. The three strong models stay flat and low across magnitude; logistic regression is high and decreasing.*

### C. Recalibration Transfer

The strong models arrive well calibrated, so recalibration has little to improve. Platt scaling degrades calibration even when fit on a representative set (random-forest ECE rises from 0.005 to ≈0.030, MLP from 0.004 to ≈0.031), and a Platt map fit on bright sources and applied to the faintest bin is worse still: ECE reaches 0.063 for the random forest and 0.062 for the MLP. Isotonic regression shows a milder version of the same pattern. Temperature scaling is the most transfer-robust: a bright-fit temperature leaves faint-bin ECE near its uncalibrated value (random forest 0.017, MLP 0.007). The transfer experiment therefore inverts the anticipated result: the danger is not that recalibration fails to fix a faint-end problem but that recalibration, bright-fit Platt scaling above all, introduces one, with the severity depending on the method (Fig. 3).

*Fig. 3. Recalibration transfer for the random forest. A Platt map fit on bright sources degrades at the faint end; temperature scaling transfers, staying near the uncalibrated baseline.*

### D. Decision-Relevant Selection Quality

Figure 4 recasts these calibration differences as the quality of a quasar sample selected at P(QSO) ≥ 0.9. For the well-calibrated models the achieved purity of the selected sample matches the purity its probabilities promise at every magnitude: the random forest's faint-bin sample (r in [20,22)) is 96.3% pure against a promised 96.3%, and HistGB and the MLP agree with their promised purities to within about one percentage point. The magnitude dependence appears instead in completeness, which falls toward faint sources, from ≈0.77 at r in [18,19) to ≈0.52 at r in [20,22) for the random forest and the other strong models alike, because fewer faint quasars clear the 0.9 threshold. Logistic regression is the cautionary case: at the faint end it promises 92% purity but delivers 77%, with completeness below 0.09, the failure mode calibration exists to prevent. For the strong models, recalibration changes these selections little (a bright-fit temperature trades a fraction of a percentage point of purity for a small gain in completeness), consistent with base probabilities that are already trustworthy.

*Fig. 4. Quasar selection quality at P(QSO) ≥ 0.9 for the random forest. Achieved purity tracks the promised purity across magnitude; completeness falls toward faint sources.*

## IV. Discussion and Conclusion

On SDSS photometry, the random-forest, gradient-boosted, and neural-network classifiers are well calibrated on the i.i.d. test set and stay so across the full magnitude range that erodes their faint-end accuracy. This echoes prior reports that a star/galaxy/quasar classifier can be well calibrated without explicit recalibration [12], and it contrasts with the pronounced calibration-under-shift degradation documented for deep image classifiers [5]; a plausible reason is that the photometric feature space and the tree, boosting, and MLP models used here do not become overconfident as photometric errors grow.

Three qualifications temper this conclusion. First, calibration is not uniform across models: logistic regression is both less accurate and miscalibrated, so calibration must be checked model by model. Second, the magnitude bins differ in class composition: the bright bins contain few quasars and the faint bins are quasar-dominated, a direct consequence of SDSS targeting quasars to faint magnitudes, so the magnitude axis is partly confounded with the class mix. The classwise calibration error reported in Section III-B addresses this by holding the class proportions out of the metric; the apparent improvement of logistic-regression calibration toward faint sources should in any case be read in light of the shifting composition. Third, the spectroscopic sample is magnitude-limited and target-selected, so the faint sources studied here are not a representative faint population; calibration measured on this labeled sample may be optimistic relative to the full photometric faint population an LSST-era pipeline would face.

Two practical implications follow. For sample selection, a capable, well-calibrated classifier delivers a probability-thresholded quasar sample whose purity matches its stated confidence at all magnitudes, so the probabilities can set purity targets directly; the binding constraint at faint magnitudes is completeness. For recalibration, the results counsel caution: because the base models are already calibrated, a map fit on a convenient bright subset and applied faint tends to introduce miscalibration, most of all for Platt scaling; temperature scaling is the safest of the three methods.

In conclusion, on SDSS DR17 photometry: (1) capable photometric star/galaxy/quasar classifiers are well calibrated and stay so across magnitude, with miscalibration confined to the weakest model; (2) post-hoc recalibration transfers poorly across magnitude and can degrade calibration when fit on bright sources, temperature scaling least of the three; and (3) a probability-thresholded selection keeps its promised purity at all magnitudes and pays its faint-end cost in completeness. Future work should extend the analysis to a representative faint sample and to cross-survey transfer, where genuine covariate shift may stress calibration more than the within-survey magnitude shift studied here.
